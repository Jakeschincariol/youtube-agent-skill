## Script

### BEAT 0 — Hook (0:00–0:45) · [ON SCREEN: the quote text being typed, then deleted]

Why do you keep charging the same price for work that is twice as hard?

You have raised your prices before. You know the exact feeling: you type the new number, you look at
it, you delete it, you type the old one back, and you tell yourself you'll fix it next quarter.

That is not a confidence problem. It is a quote problem — because the quote you send has one shape,
so there is only one thing you can do to it, and that thing is uncomfortable.

So here are seven levers. Not mindset. Mechanical changes to the quote and the call, ranked by how
fast they pay. Lever one pays on your next invoice. Lever seven takes a quarter, and then keeps
paying while you sleep.

Pull three of them before your next client call, and you will not type the old number again.

Lever one.

### BEAT 1 — Lever 1: the renewal letter (0:45–2:10) · [ON SCREEN: "LEVER 1 / THE RENEWAL LETTER" · a one-page letter]

The fastest money in your business is already in your inbox, from somebody who has paid you before.

Every client whose rate you have not touched in a year is a discount you gave away by accident. So
you send a letter. One page, no apology, three parts. What you have delivered for them. What the
rate is going up to. And the date it starts.

Then you add the part everybody forgets: what is not changing. Turnaround time. The thing they
actually hired you for. That sentence is what turns a price rise into a piece of news instead of a
piece of conflict.

Two rules. First, the increase is a number, not a vibe. Not "around ten percent" — ten percent,
effective the first of next month. Second, you give notice, not permission. You are not asking.
You are telling them early, in writing, so they can plan.

Most of them will reply "that's fine". One of them will leave, and that is the lever working. If
nobody has ever left over a price rise, you waited a year too long, and you have been paying for
that patience out of your own pocket.

### BEAT 2 — Lever 2: sell the date, not the work (2:10–3:20) · [ON SCREEN: same project, two delivery dates, two prices]

Nobody buys the same work at the same price. They buy it at a date.

You have been quoting one timeline, because it is the comfortable one for you — the one with slack in
it and no late nights. Offer the date they actually want instead, and charge for the difference. The
same project, delivered in half the time, at one and a half times the price.

Call it priority work. Not a rush fee — rush sounds like you failed to plan, and clients negotiate
with people who failed to plan. Priority sounds like a queue, and queues have prices.

Then hold the boundary out loud, on the call: priority means this is first, and something else moves
back. If you take the priority job and nothing moves, you have sold the same hours twice, and you
have bought yourself a terrible week for the privilege.

### BEAT 3 — Lever 3: three prices beat one (3:20–4:35) · [ON SCREEN: quote with one line → quote with three]

One price invites one question: can you do better on the price?

Three prices invite a completely different one: which one?

So the quote becomes three lines. A smaller scope. The work they asked for. And a version with the
thing you wish they would buy. The middle one is what you actually want to sell, and it gets chosen
because the third one made it look reasonable.

Two rules. The cheapest option has to be real — a straw man gets spotted, and then everything you
send afterwards is suspect. And all three solve the same problem at a different depth. Now the call
is not about your rate. It is about their scope, which is the only conversation where you win.

### BEAT 4 — Lever 4: downsize, never discount (4:35–5:50) · [ON SCREEN: price stays, scope shrinks]

Every discount teaches a client one thing: the first number was not true. And a client who learns
that will ask again, next time, because you taught them to.

So when the budget does not fit, take something out. Never the rate — the scope. Remove the round of
revisions. Remove the second deliverable. Remove the thing they assumed was included. Same day rate.
Smaller box.

Say it like this: "I can't lower the rate, because the rate is what the work costs. But here is the
version that fits your budget."

And then watch. Plenty of them say "actually, put the revisions back". That sentence was in the room
the whole time. The discount was the only thing standing in front of it.

### BEAT 5 — Lever 5: charge for the invisible hours (5:50–7:05) · [ON SCREEN: timeline, most of it grey and unpaid]

You are charging for the hours that can be seen. The building, the shooting, the design, the writing.
And you are giving away the hours that decide whether any of it works.

The kickoff call. Reading their documents. The nineteen questions you ask before you start. That is
not admin. That is the reason the project does not fail in week three.

So it gets a name and a price. A paid kickoff. A paid audit. A paid first week. And the moment it is
paid, two things change at once: they show up prepared, and the free consulting stops.

This one also filters. Some people will not pay to start. Those were the people who were going to be
the project that never ends, and you just found out for the price of one email.

### BEAT 6 — Lever 6: price the outcome, not the clock (7:05–8:15) · [ON SCREEN: hours × rate → fixed price for a fixed result]

Hourly pricing has one fatal property: the better you get, the less you earn. Solve in a day what
used to take two, and the invoice gets smaller. You are being billed for the problem, not the fix.

So you price the thing, not the time. But pick the version you can defend. Not "I'm charging for the
value I create" — that is a speech, and it arrives out of nowhere on a Tuesday. Instead: a fixed
price for a fixed outcome, scoped in writing, revisions capped, with a number that does not move.

That is how you get paid for being fast instead of punished for it. And on the day your estimate is
wrong, you eat it. Once. That is the tuition for the next seven deals.

### BEAT 7 — Lever 7: sell the same build twice (8:15–9:15) · [ON SCREEN: one build, three buyers]

Everything you build for one client that is not specific to them is an asset you are giving away. The
template. The checklist. The process. The thirty minutes you repeat at the start of every single
project.

Lever seven is selling it again. To a second client, as a productised version. To a group, as a small
workshop. Or to everyone, as the thing in your bio that people buy before they ever email you. Same
build, second sale, no extra hours.

This is the slowest lever, because it needs the first version finished and the second buyer to exist.
But it is the only one on the list where the second sale costs you nothing extra. Stack two of those
and you have changed the shape of a month.

### BEAT 8 — The one to pull (9:15–10:00) · [ON SCREEN: seven levers, one highlighted]

Seven levers. And the worst thing you can do is pull all seven at once, because then you cannot tell
which one worked, and you will be back to guessing.

Pick one. Pull it on your next quote, or in your next email, this week. Then keep it, and add the
second.

If you have ten minutes today, it is lever one — the renewal letter — because you already have the
clients, you already have the inbox, and nobody has to say yes to anything for it to start.

The full script, the chapter list, and the exact wording for that letter are in the description.

And if you pull one of these and it works, come back and tell me which one. That is the comment
section I want to read.

## Why this order

Ranked by time-to-cash, not by importance: invoice → next quote → next project → next quarter →
compounding. A listicle that ranks by something real survives the retention cliff at item four,
because the viewer can see the ordering rule and knows what is left.
